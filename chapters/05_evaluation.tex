\section{Empirical Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We present the empirical evaluation of the routing engine configurations on the Moscow road network. We evaluate seven priority queue structures (excluding the duplicate Delta queue in favor of the identical Bucket queue) across three shortest path algorithms with $1 + 2 \times 5$ weight variations (unweighted Dijkstra, and A* and ALT swept over five heuristic weights $w \in [1.0, 1.2]$). First, we analyze execution time composition using our multivariate OLS regression. 
Second, we validate performance significance with ANOVA. 
Third, we outline the three-step filtering pipeline to isolate the optimal configurations.

\subsection{Execution Latency Decomposition}
Our OLS regression models query latency $T_{\text{ms}}$ against visited nodes $N_{\text{visited}}$, queue execution time $T_{\text{queue}}$, and pop cycles $C_{\text{pop}}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\text{ms}} = & 0.9921 - (8.46 \times 10^{-5}) \cdot N_{\text{visited}} \\
& + 3.3214 \cdot T_{\text{queue}} - 0.0082 \cdot C_{\text{pop}}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ols_formula}
\end{equation}
with $R^2 = 0.998$ and $F = 2.041 \times 10^{5}$ ($p < 10^{-3}$). The $T_{\text{queue}}$ coefficient of 3.3214 confirms that queue operations scale query latency linearly. The negative coefficient for $C_{\text{pop}}$ reflects per-cycle savings from the SIMD down-heap: Simd8AryHeap reduces pop-path length, so more CPU cycles spent per pop correspond to fewer total pop operations. ANOVA results verify that the algorithm, queue layout, and path edge count all significantly affect execution time: $F_{\text{Algorithm}} = 1305.63$, $F_{\text{Queue}} = 30.76$, and $F_{\text{PathEdges}} = 7691.36$ ($p < 10^{-40}$). Path length dominates, confirming that topological complexity drives the execution budget, while queue selection shapes the constant factor.
\subsection{Macro-Topological Search Space Scaling and Isomorphism Zones}
To prevent visual clutter and evaluate the full matrix of configurations simultaneously, we reject discrete line plotting in favor of continuous behavioral bands (Figure~\ref{fig:algo_bands}). We map all seven candidate priority queues over three routing algorithms, covering $1 + 2 \times 5$ parameter variations. The duplicate Delta queue is explicitly excluded in favor of the identical Bucket queue. Each band represents an algorithmic paradigm partitioned by its empirical scaling boundaries: the upper limit is dictated by the scalar 2-ary baseline heap, while the lower bound is constrained by our vectorized SIMD architectures. Despite minor overlap in the mid-range path bins, the ALT band demonstrates clear performance superiority over A* and Dijkstra throughout the entire path complexity range.

The empirical evaluation isolates a strict microarchitectural isomorphism across the entire evaluation space. The baseline unweighted Dijkstra framework occupies a distinct upper latency zone with a scaling exponent of $\alpha \approx 2.09$ across all tested heaps. The weighted $A^*$ continuum ($w \in [1.0, 1.2]$) shifts into a wider parallel band ($\alpha \approx 2.45$), heavily penalized by the bidirectional exploration of the unconstrained scale-free network frontier. 

The directed ALT framework ($w \in [1.0, 1.2]$) compresses the active corridor, mapping to the lowest latency band with $\alpha \approx 1.86$. The invariant geometric parallel topology of these three bands empirically proves that priority queue mutations shift only the absolute performance constant ($k$), while the macro-topological structure of the heuristic determines the asymptotic growth law ($\alpha$).

\subsection{Parametric Elasticity and Monotonicity Breakdown Analysis}
The structural response to heuristic weight inflation ($w > 1.0$) uncovers a radical divergence between coordinate-free and interval-bound priority queues. For the vectorized $A^*$ continuum, increasing the weight exhibits a positive throughput elasticity ($\varepsilon_w$). The inflated heuristic pulls the active vertex exploration frontier into a highly directional, narrow corridor. Because the spatial heuristic gradient remains stable, vertex keys enter the queue in a mostly monotone sequence, minimizing deep down-heap swaps.

Conversely, for the ALT framework running on interval-restricted architectures like the \texttt{SimdRadixHeap}, weight inflation triggers an absolute performance collapse, with elasticity dropping sharply to $\varepsilon_w = -3.58$ at $w=1.05$. This phenomenon is not tied to standard memory overhead, but to the destruction of the key monotonicity invariant. The inflated ALT potential formula:
\begin{equation}
f(v) = g(v) + w \cdot h(v)
\end{equation}
becomes non-admissible and non-monotone when $w > 1.0$, causing downstream vertices to generate lower keys than their parents. As a result, elements drift backward into previously closed buckets, forcing the \texttt{SimdRadixHeap} to halt its $O(1)$ extraction loop and execute exhaustive, branch-heavy bucket-scan recovery passes.

\subsection{The Three-Step Filtering Pipeline}

To identify the optimal routing configuration, we establish a three-step filtering pipeline that discards suboptimal search space and memory layout variants.

\subsubsection{Step 1: Algorithmic Filtering (Search Envelope Exponent)}
We isolate the relationship between path length $L$ and search latency $T_{\text{ms}}$ using a power-law model $T_{\text{ms}} = k \cdot L^{\alpha}$. 
Dijkstra routing explores a broad circular envelope, producing a complexity exponent of $\alpha = 2.0947$ ($R^2 = 0.7724$). 
A* search with $w=1.0$ exhibits directed search but suffers from high exploration width, yielding $\alpha = 2.4506$ ($R^2 = 0.8389$). 
ALT routing with $w=1.0$ restricts the search space to a narrow corridor, achieving $\alpha = 1.8690$ ($R^2 = 0.4889$). 

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity} illustrates this microarchitectural isomorphism. 
Within each algorithm, all eight queue backends produce nearly identical $\alpha$ values: the spread is $\pm 0.03$ for Dijkstra and $\pm 0.03$ for A*, and $\pm 0.06$ for ALT. 
The priority queue acts as a multiplicative constant $k$ while the search algorithm determines the exponent $\alpha$. 
Figure~\ref{fig:elasticity} shows that A* and Dijkstra carry positive but modest QPS elasticity across weight levels, while ALT exhibits strong sensitivity: the $\alpha$ exponent falls to 1.69 for RadixHeap, the lowest of all tested configurations. 
We filter Dijkstra and A* due to their excessive search envelopes.

\subsubsection{Step 2: Weight Constraint and Monotonicity Filtering}
Two failure modes disqualify $w > 1.0$.

\textbf{Accuracy bound.} 
At $w=1.2$, paths exceeding 450 edges carry a mean relative error of 9.8\% and produce zero exact-match results. 
A production routing engine cannot accept this error regime.

\textbf{Radix monotonicity collapse.} 
ALT with $w > 1.0$ emits non-monotone priority keys: the inflated heuristic $f(v) = g(v) + w \cdot h(v)$ can decrease for nodes already in the open set, violating RadixHeap's strict monotonicity invariant. 
Each key violation triggers an $O(B)$ bucket-scan recovery pass. 
Figure~\ref{fig:radix_collapse} exposes the consequence: SimdRadixHeap mean latency climbs from 2.94\,ms at $w=1.0$ to 22.66\,ms at $w=1.2$, representing a $7.7\times$ explosion. 
Ghost-node density falls from 0.456 to 0.089 in parallel: the search frontier is corrupted before it expands fully, so fewer productive nodes enter the queue. 
Figure~\ref{fig:reopen} shows the re-opening index $\rho = N_{\text{pop}} / N_{\text{visited}}$ for RadixHeap \emph{drops} from 1.146 to 1.022 as $w$ increases, because the search terminates prematurely rather than correctly. 
The QPS elasticity for RadixHeap is $-3.58$ at $w=1.05$; for Simd8AryHeap it is $+7.27$. 
We fix $w = 1.0$ for all remaining evaluation.

\subsubsection{Step 3: Tail Latency Stability Filtering}
With $w=1.0$ fixed, we evaluate hardware stability using excess kurtosis and tail roughness $p_{99}/p_{50}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:kurtosis} reports excess kurtosis per queue. 
SimdBucketQueue records excess kurtosis of 41.2 and roughness 12.9, meaning one in thirteen queries runs 12$\times$ slower than the median. 
The SimdDeltaQueue implementation is identical in observed metrics (kurtosis 41.8, roughness 12.9) and is excluded as a duplicate. 
This variance in SimdBucketQueue arises from bucket-scan overhead on long paths, which grows with path complexity and produces a heavy upper tail. 
SimdQuickHeap records kurtosis 16.0 and roughness 9.1, elevated by branch mispredictions in its lazy sifting routine. 
Figure~\ref{fig:roughness} shows Simd8AryHeap achieves the most stable profile: kurtosis 12.4 and roughness 8.5. 
SimdRadixHeap records kurtosis 20.1 and roughness 9.0. 
We eliminate SimdBucketQueue, SimdDeltaQueue, and SimdQuickHeap from the candidate set. Both Simd8AryHeap and SimdRadixHeap qualify for final throughput evaluation.

This exact filtering process highlights a major methodological advancement over our previous simulation iterations. In our initial experiments, we relied on an approximate Delta-queue layout (with a coarse bucket width $\Delta$) to achieve rapid routing cycles, which came at the cost of a 1.29\% Mean Relative Error (MRE) in path stretch. By transitioning to a vectorized, exact 8-ary priority heap (\texttt{Simd8AryHeap}) with bit-packed coordinate-key fields, we successfully restore absolute path optimality (0\% stretch error) while matching or exceeding the query throughput of the approximate structure.

\subsection{Journey Profile Clustering}

K-Means clustering groups the 1500 benchmark runs into three spatial profiles. 
Figure~\ref{fig:cluster_qps} plots QPS against path length in edges for all candidate queues at ALT $w=1.0$. 
The three shaded regions correspond to the K-Means cluster boundaries.

In the Short-Range region (Cluster 2, 30--150 edges, mean 12.3\,km), SimdRadixHeap achieves 32,522~QPS on 30--60 edge paths and 4,284~QPS on 60--100 edge paths --- the highest values across all queues. 
Simd8AryHeap trails at 27,572 and 3,240~QPS respectively for the same bins. 
In the Long-Range region (Cluster 1, above 300 edges, mean 43.7\,km), queue operations dominate the execution budget. 
SimdRadixHeap maintains 502~QPS on 300--450 edge paths and 270~QPS on 450--600 edge paths; Simd8AryHeap reaches 328 and 228~QPS. 
SimdBucketQueue occupies second place at 300--450 edges (441~QPS) but holds no advantage over SimdRadixHeap at any bin. 
SimdRadixHeap leads across all three cluster profiles; its advantage over Simd8AryHeap grows from $18\%$ for short-range queries to $53\%$ for long-range journeys --- consistent with its lower complexity exponent $\alpha = 1.69$ identified in Step~1.
